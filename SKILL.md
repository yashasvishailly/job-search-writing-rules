# Job search writing rules

This is a deny list. It says what never goes into a job search message, and what goes in place of it.

Most job search advice tells you what to say. Nobody tells you what to delete. The deleting is where the replies come from.

## How to use it

Write the draft first. Then run every rule below as a separate pass over that draft. A line that trips a rule gets cut. Softening it does not count.

When you run this as a Claude skill, do the same thing out loud: produce the draft, audit it rule by rule, and report which lines you removed and why. Do not hand back a draft you haven't audited.

## Rule 1: never name a gap you can't close

The job asks for a school, a degree, a number of years, a visa, a named tool. You don't have it. You do not mention it.

No "I'm not from IIT, but". No "I don't have the 8 years, however". No "while I lack a formal background in".

Apply anyway. Write only what you do have.

The sentence that raises the objection is the sentence that screens you out. The reader wasn't thinking about it. You wrote it down for them. A filter you argue with in writing is a filter you just confirmed applies to you.

One exception. A hard constraint the employer hits on day one (work authorization, notice period, where you physically live) gets stated once, plainly, with no apology and no argument attached. State it. Don't defend it.

## Rule 2: no hedging vocabulary

Banned outright:

if, but, just, maybe, perhaps, might, hopefully, possibly, somewhat, kind of, a bit, I think, I believe, I feel like, I was wondering, I'd love the chance to, would it be possible, happy to, feel free to

"If you're open to it, I'd love to chat" becomes "Here's the first thing I'd take on."

"I just wanted to reach out about" gets deleted. Start at the point.

Hedges ask for permission to have standing. You're requesting that before you've said anything worth standing on.

## Rule 3: no research theatre

Never pitch their own company, product, funding round, thesis, portfolio, or press back at them. They were there. They know.

One clause on how you found them is the ceiling.

Cut: "I've been following Acme since your Series A and I love what you're building in vertical SaaS."

Keep: "Saw your post about the ops hire." Then move.

Reciting a founder's company to the founder proves one thing, which is that you can read a homepage.

## Rule 4: point the experience at their problem

Don't list what you did. Don't recite what they do. Join the two. Because I've done X, I can do Y here.

Weak: "I scaled a team from 7 to 22 and cut invoice delays by 50%."

Strong: "The last team I joined went from 7 people to 22 in a year. The first thing that broke was billing, and I cut invoice delays in half. You're hiring at the same headcount."

## Rule 5: blame the process, never the person

Every problem you name is a system problem. Never a verdict on the reader, their hire, their team, or a call they already made.

Weak: "Your onboarding is broken."

Strong: "Onboarding is the first thing that breaks at 20 people. It breaks everywhere."

A stranger who opens with a judgment of you is a stranger you don't answer. Relief gets replies.

## Rule 6: the opening line is a fact, not a confident guess

Anything you assert about their business has to be checkable and sourced, written in plain English.

Banned: "Things fall through the cracks at your stage." That is speculation wearing a confident voice.

Banned: any statistic you can't cite to a real source. No invented benchmarks, no rounded-off numbers you half remember.

No real fact behind the angle means the angle goes, not that you dress it up.

## Rule 7: fabricate nothing

No invented anecdote. No metric you didn't produce. No mutual contact you don't have. No reason for writing that isn't the reason.

If a detail would make the message better and it isn't true, the message is worse with it.

## Rule 8: use the words for what you actually did

Don't borrow a hotter label than the work. Revenue operations is not "growth". Coordinating a calendar is not "chief of staff". Answering tickets is not "customer success strategy".

Real titles. Real dates. Company names written in full, every time, every document.

The reader hires for the literal thing and checks.

## Rule 9: cut your backstory

Your history tells you what the message should argue. It is not the content of the message.

Out: origin story, "I've always been passionate about", the career arc, why you're looking, what happened at the last place.

The reader is deciding whether to spend fifteen minutes on you. They are not reading a memoir.

## Rule 10: length

A DM is three to four sentences. The ask or the link sits alone on the final line.

A cover letter fits on one screen without scrolling. Three short paragraphs.

An application box answers the question asked, then stops. No bonus material.

## Rule 11: formatting

No em dashes anywhere.

No bold, no headers, no bullet points, no markdown, no emoji, no hashtags inside a DM or an email. Plain sentences.

Use contractions. Vary sentence length. Three parallel clauses in a row reads as machine writing, and so does every sentence landing at the same length.

## Rule 12: no "X, not Y"

Never the contrast construction. State the positive claim and stop.

Cut: "This isn't about tools. It's about ownership."

Write: "Ownership is the thing that's missing."

Also banned: "not just X but Y", "less X, more Y", "X? No. Y."

## Rule 13: banned vocabulary and openers

See `banned-words.md`. Every word and phrase on that list is an AI tell, a filler, or both. None of them survive the pass.

## Rule 14: keep it about the work

Nothing in a cover letter or a DM reads as personal interest in the reader. No compliments about them as a person, their photo, their energy, their vibe.

Talk about the work.

## The pass before you send

Read the draft once for each of these. Answer yes or no.

1. Did I name a requirement I don't meet?
2. Is there an "if", a "but", or a "just" anywhere?
3. Did I describe their company back to them for more than one clause?
4. Does every claim about my experience connect to something they need?
5. Have I judged a person instead of a process?
6. Can I source every fact and every number?
7. Is anything in here untrue?
8. Am I using a label for my work that inflates it?
9. Is any of my backstory still in the draft?
10. Is it short enough that they'll finish it?
11. Any em dashes, bold, bullets, or emoji?
12. Any "X, not Y" constructions?
13. Any banned words left?

One no on any of these means the line goes.
